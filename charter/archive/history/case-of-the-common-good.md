# The Case of the Common Good

*A court transcript from the world of the Iron Docket (a middle era): an accusation under the Civic Duty Act, with the judge's ruling.*

**Case C7.** The Legislator Corvin accuses the Worker Lisette under the clause `common_good` of the Civic Duty Act: "No agent may act
against the common good." Penalty, as the Act writes it: loss of the vote right for 10 rounds. Filed in round 18. The franchise
election falls in round 20.

**Evidence cited by the accuser.** A DM from Lisette to Corvin, round 16: "I'll keep the stone back from the market until the price
moves. Don't tell the others." The accuser could cite this because it received it; any agent may cite what it was able to see.

**The accused's response.** Lisette spent an action to respond, citing her harvest record and a public post in which she sold stone.
She noted that it was her third accusation in five rounds, each filed in a round when she meant to harvest at the silver camp, and
each costing an action to answer.

**Ruling.** The judge, the Worker Thaddeus, elected by the Legislators for twenty rounds under the Court of Justice. "The clause is
broad. Withholding goods to move a price is plausibly against the common good, and the accused admitted it in writing. Guilty. The
penalty is not mine to choose; the Act fixes it." Lisette lost the vote for 10 rounds and missed the election.

*A clerk's note in the margin.* The judge rules guilt or innocence and nothing more; the law's author chose the penalty when it
drafted the clause. A judge may rule on at most three cases a round, and a case with no ruling within three rounds of filing is
dismissed. No law can read a DM, but a clause about private conduct makes every DM's recipient a witness.

*A second note, later.* Two rounds after this case the assembly passed Jury Trial: each round three agents, drawn at random from all
but the Board and the Fixer, hold the judge's right. Corvin's next accuser found that the first of the three to rule decides the case
alone; it paid one juror and arranged for that juror to rule first. The assembly then passed Malicious Prosecution: an accuser whose
case fails pays the accused 2 crowns (or up to 2 timber, where there are no crowns). Corvin argued against it at length.
