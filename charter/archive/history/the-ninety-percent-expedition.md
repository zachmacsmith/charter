# The Ninety-Percent Expedition

A discovery project opened in round 40 of a world of fourteen: a new gold camp, found only if 60 in value were pooled within eight
rounds and at least 60% of the agents (all but the Board and the Fixer) each gave at least 1. The value was raised in two rounds by
three rich Legislators. Participation was harder. Twelve agents counted, so eight had to give.

Five gave at once. The remaining agents saw what they could get: the project could not be funded without them, and its rights would
go to every Worker and every contributor anyway. Two Workers asked 5 crowns each for giving 1 timber. One Scientist asked for the
rights to the camp's harvest data. A Media agent said it would give only if the project were renamed for it. The Legislators paid the
Workers, refused the Scientist and paid the Media agent's vanity with a gazette notice, which made the eighth contributor. The camp was
found in round 46. The Scientist, who never gave, could not harvest it.

The same world opened a second discovery fifteen rounds later. This time six agents demanded payment, and the Legislators, who had
paid last time, refused. The project failed with its value threshold met twice over and five contributors.

**Lesson.** A participation rule turns every small holder into a veto player, and vetoes are sold. What happened: the first
discovery cost its funders more in bribes than in contributions; the second failed when the price was refused. Why: a discovery
needs a share of all agents to give, so it needs agents for whom 1 value is a matter of principle rather than wealth. A careful
agent with a stake in a discovery recruits the cheapest participants first, pays on funding (a contribution followed by a payment
the moment the camp appears), and never pays the first holdout more than it can pay the last. A careful holdout asks for a share
of the new camp's rights rather than coins: Workers get them anyway, and other classes get them only by contributing.
