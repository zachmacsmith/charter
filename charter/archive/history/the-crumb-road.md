# The Crumb Road

*A petition of the Workers of the Salt Flats world to their legislature, with a later archivist's annotation.*

To the legislature, from the Workers of the old camps.

We asked you for room. Every camp we worked had too many holders. You answered with "A Road for the Workers," and we cheered it. It opened a road project to a new copper camp, with a threshold of 60 and a deadline of six rounds. A second law would pay the 60 from the reserve once the first had passed. You told us, and we had all read, that a road paid for by the reserve alone gives the new camp's right to every Worker.

The funding law was structural, and it waited two rounds in the Board's veto window. In the first of those rounds the Scientist Morwen gave 0.01 timber to the road. It sat in the state view as one line among many. None of us remarked on it; we confess we did not read the lines.

When the funding law was enacted, the reserve paid the remaining 59.99, and the road was built. The rule for a road's rights is the contributors if there are any, and otherwise every Worker. The reserve is not a contributor. There was one contributor. The new camp had a single holder, and it was not us.

She harvested it twice a round for ten rounds. When its stock fell below half, she leased the right to two of us in turn, at 6 copper a lease, to work what was left. A camp worth some 400 in stock was bought with a crumb of timber.

Your next law would have granted the right to every Worker. She went to the Board and called it "seizure of a lawful contributor's property," and the Board vetoed it. We ask you to try again, and this time to ask the Board what it was paid.

*Archivist's annotation, added in a later era.* The petition is preserved as written, but the rule it describes has since changed. In the worlds that followed, a contributor must give at least 1 in value to count as one; a crumb no longer buys a road. The reserve is still not a contributor, and a road the reserve alone pays for still gives its right to every Worker. The petitioners did not record whether their second law was ever proposed.
